## 1 · Control of hormone release — the three stimuli

**Flagged:** 10 Sept 2026, off her slide.

### Likely wording

> Describe the control of hormone secretion according to the three mechanisms
> of hormonal regulation. Give an example of each. **(6 marks)**

A "…and feedback cycles" tail is likely, because her learning objective joins
the two. If it appears, add the feedback step below.

### Model answer — one mark per line

1. **Humoral** — a chemical in the blood is itself the stimulus for release of the hormone.
2. e.g. a fall in blood Ca²⁺ stimulates release of PTH from the parathyroid gland; blood glucose levels act as the stimulus for insulin and glucagon from the pancreas.
3. **Neural** — the nervous system directly stimulates the gland to release its hormone.
4. e.g. activation of the sympathetic nervous system releases adrenaline from the adrenal medulla; suckling makes the posterior pituitary release oxytocin.
5. **Hormonal** — a hormone from one endocrine gland stimulates another endocrine gland to release its hormone.
6. e.g. TSH from the anterior pituitary stimulates release of thyroxine from the thyroid gland; ACTH → cortisol from the adrenal cortex; FSH → oestrogen from the ovary.

If feedback is asked for as well:

7. Nearly all of it runs on **negative feedback** — the rising hormone or the corrected blood level switches the original stimulus off, holding the variable at its set point (rising Ca²⁺ shuts down PTH; rising thyroxine shuts down TSH).

### The trap

Her distractor list always carries a fourth option that does not exist:
**"Enzyme" / "Enzymatic" stimulation**. There are three mechanisms, not four.

## 2 · Label the endocrine glands on a diagram

**Flagged:** 10 Sept 2026, from her lecture.

### Likely wording

> Label the endocrine glands A–J on the diagram. **(5 marks)**

Her warm-up exercise used the same figure with letters A–G and the lab book has
the same picture. Expect half a mark per label, and expect the pituitary to be a
single label (on a whole-body figure you cannot tell anterior from posterior; she
said so at 37:04).

### Model answer — what each label needs

1. **Hypothalamus** — floor of the diencephalon; an endocrine gland in its own right, "the master gland" (57:37, 58:44).
2. **Pituitary** — below the hypothalamus on its stalk (the infundibulum), in the sella turcica of the sphenoid.
3. **Pineal** — epithalamus, posterior diencephalon; melatonin.
4. **Thyroid** — anterior neck, below the larynx.
5. **Parathyroids** — four, on the posterior surface of the thyroid (she said you would not have to label these on the body figure, 38:23).
6. **Thymus** — behind the sternum, above the heart.
7. **Adrenals** — one on top of each kidney; cortex outside, medulla inside.
8. **Pancreas** — behind the stomach; islets of Langerhans are the endocrine part.
9. **Ovaries** — pelvis. 10. **Testes** — scrotum.

### The traps

- On the pituitary close-up she will ask *which lobe is this and why*: blood
  vessels (the portal system) mean **anterior**; nerve fibres mean **posterior**.
  *"You don't have to know how the person is sitting… you can see on the blood
  vessels and the nerves."* [1:15:14–1:16:32]
- "Two glands in one": adrenal cortex vs medulla, thyroid follicular vs
  parafollicular cells [38:23].
- Hormone-making organs that are **not** endocrine glands, which she used as MCQ
  bait: kidney (erythropoietin), heart atria (ANP), stomach (gastrin, secretin),
  adipose (oestrogen), placenta [1:13:19–1:15:14].

### Why it's likely

## 3 · Calcium regulation — PTH and calcitonin

**Flagged:** 10 Sept 2026, from her lecture (1:30:47) and the formative test
(1:33:21). Her Practice SAQ page already carries the wording.

### Likely wording

> Explain how the parathyroid gland helps to regulate calcium in the body when
> calcium is low. **(4 marks)**

or the musculoskeletal version from the same page:

> Explain in detail how calcium is regulated by changing the activity of certain
> bone cells. Remember to include the hormones involved in this regulation.
> **(3 marks)**

### Model answer — one mark per line

1. Low blood Ca²⁺ is a **humoral** stimulus: it acts directly on the **chief cells of the parathyroid glands** (four, posterior thyroid), which secrete **parathyroid hormone (PTH)**.
2. PTH stimulates **osteoclasts** to digest bone matrix and release Ca²⁺ into the blood.
3. PTH stimulates the **kidneys** to reabsorb Ca²⁺ (and secrete phosphate).
4. PTH increases absorption of Ca²⁺ by the **intestinal** mucosal cells.
5. Blood Ca²⁺ rises; rising Ca²⁺ **inhibits** PTH release — negative feedback.
6. The opposing arm: high blood Ca²⁺ stimulates the **parafollicular cells of the thyroid** to release **calcitonin**, which inhibits osteoclasts and stimulates Ca²⁺ deposition into bone matrix (osteoblasts), lowering blood Ca²⁺. Calcitonin is PTH's antagonist.

### The traps

- Name the **cells** and the gland, not just the gland (her insulin rule at 1:38:50 applies here word for word).
- Three PTH targets, not one: bone, kidney, intestine.
- PTH and calcitonin are peptides (water-soluble, membrane receptors); do not call
  them steroids.

### Why it's likely

- *"The short answer questions that will come from this section, I would say would
  be calcium control and sugar control because they are a little bit more of a
  story."* [1:30:47]
- The formative test already has it at 3.5 marks plus a 2.5-mark calcitonin item [1:33:21].
- Learning outcome 8 for the endocrine block names the parathyroid explicitly.

## 4 · Blood glucose regulation — insulin and glucagon

**Flagged:** 10 Sept 2026, from her lecture (1:30:47, 1:33:21). Taught in full on
11 Sept (Endocrine 2, online).

### Likely wording

> Fully explain how the pancreas regulates blood sugar levels in the body.
> **(4 marks)**

That is verbatim from her Practice SAQ page. "Fully" means both arms and the
feedback.

### Model answer — one mark per line

1. High blood glucose is a **humoral** stimulus: the **beta cells of the islets of Langerhans in the pancreas** secrete **insulin**.
2. Insulin stimulates muscle and fat cells to **absorb glucose** from the blood, and liver cells to form glycogen from glucose (**glycogenesis**); blood glucose falls.
3. Low blood glucose stimulates the **alpha cells of the islets** to secrete **glucagon**.
4. Glucagon stimulates liver cells to break glycogen into glucose (**glycogenolysis**) and to make new glucose from non-carbohydrate sources (**gluconeogenesis**); blood glucose rises.
5. Each hormone's effect removes its own stimulus — **negative feedback** — so the two opposing effects hold blood glucose at a stable state: homeostasis.

### The traps

- *"Pancreas won't even get a mark."* Beta cells, islets of Langerhans, pancreas; alpha cells, islets of Langerhans, pancreas. [1:38:50]
- Both hormones end in **-in / -on** and are water-soluble peptides: membrane receptors, cascade in the cytoplasm — not gene activation.
- **Growth hormone also raises blood glucose** (inhibits cellular uptake); she flagged it as a note-taker [1:18:52]. Cortisol and adrenaline do too, if a "which hormones raise blood glucose" MCQ appears.
- The islets are the endocrine part; the rest of the pancreas is exocrine (digestive enzymes, ducted).

### Why it's likely

- Her words at 1:30:47 and *"tomorrow we will do the story of sugar regulation, so
  then you will be able to do this question"* [1:33:21] about the formative test.
- The exam case study is on this (see entry 5).

## 5 · The metabolic word set

**Flagged:** 10 Sept 2026, from her lecture (1:40:37–1:46:22).

### Likely wording

> Define the following terms: glycogenesis, glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis,
> lipogenesis, lipolysis. **(5 marks)**

or embedded as steps inside the sugar-regulation SAQ or the exam case study.

### Model answer — one mark per line, built the way she built it

- *-genesis* = to make; *-lysis* = to break down; *neo-* = new.
1. **Glycogenesis** — making glycogen from glucose (liver, under insulin, when blood glucose is high).
2. **Glycogenolysis** — breaking glycogen down to glucose (liver, under glucagon, when blood glucose is low).
3. **Gluconeogenesis** — making new glucose from something that was never glucose, e.g. amino acids (glucagon).
4. **Lipogenesis** — making fat (insulin, when glucose is in excess).
5. **Lipolysis** — breaking fat down (for fuel; growth hormone and glucagon).

### The why she wants with it

Glucose is a monomer, sweet and **osmotically active**, so it pulls water and
dehydrates cells; glycogen is a large polymer that is neither, so the body stores
glucose as glycogen and releases it back as glucose when it needs energy
[1:45:14–1:46:22].

### Why it's likely

- *"This is also an exam question, your case study is also about this… you must
  know these words for the exam and test."* [1:46:22]
- Her Endocrine 2 deck has a slide of nothing but these words (slide 26).
